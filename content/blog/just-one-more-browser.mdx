# The tabs made me do it

I never meant to become a browser nomad. Most people pick Chrome once and call it a day. For developers, though, the browser isn’t just “where the internet lives” — it’s a core piece of the workflow. The right browser can feel like an extension of your IDE. The wrong one? A constant source of friction.

For me, the cracks first showed up in Chrome. On paper it did everything right — stable, synced across devices, and the de facto default. But small things started to grate. Like watching 1080p60 YouTube at 2× speed and seeing frames drop on a Ryzen laptop that was supposed to be more than capable. Not the end of the world, but enough to ask the dangerous question: could another browser actually do this better?

That was the start of the journey — one hopeful install after another, chasing small wins in speed, tab management, and developer ergonomics. Some stuck for a while, others fell apart quickly. This post is my attempt to unpack that pattern: what each browser got right, where they broke down, and why I keep jumping ship every few months.

## The browser you already have

When I first started using Chrome, I wasn’t a real software engineer yet - just a high school student with a new laptop and way too many YouTube tabs open. Chrome fit that stage of life perfectly: simple, reliable, and the obvious choice everyone around me used.

For a while, I didn’t think much about browsers at all. I wasn’t exploring extensions or power features yet. Chrome was just “the place the internet lived.” But over time, cracks started to show. Watching YouTube at 2x speed, something I did constantly, started to feel clunky. Frames would drop for me even on decent hardware. It was a small annoyance, but annoying enough to make me wonder if another browser could do better.

## A gamer’s Browser, apparently

With Chrome dropping frames during something as simple as watching YouTube, I started looking around for alternatives. Around this time OperaGX was everywhere — sponsoring half the YouTubers I followed, with the bold pitch of being “a browser for gamers.” It was the first time I’d seen a browser marketed as anything other than a boring utility, and the idea caught me.

To my surprise, it actually worked. OperaGX fixed the YouTube issue instantly — I could finally watch videos at 2× speed without stutter. It even felt snappier at first: tabs opened quickly, searches felt faster, and for a while I genuinely believed their performance claims.

But the honeymoon period didn’t last. The longer I used OperaGX, the slower it got, until I was right back where I started with Chrome — dropped frames, lag, and general clunkiness. On top of that, every update seemed to pile on more unnecessary features: built-in games, sidebars for Instagram, Facebook, Twitter… things I never asked for and could just open in a tab anyway. The browser went from fresh and exciting to bloated and frustrating.

So, once again, I got curious — maybe it was time to give Chrome another shot.

## Performance, profiles, and pretending it’s new

Back to Chrome it was. This time, though, it felt different. Suddenly it was the fast browser — tabs opened quickly, YouTube ran smoothly, and startup times were snappy. After OperaGX’s bloated mess, Chrome felt refreshingly simple and intuitive. Maybe that’s what I had been missing all along.

I stuck with Chrome for quite a while, though at first I used it in the same boring way I always had. That changed when I started my first jobs in the software industry. Suddenly my browser wasn’t just for watching YouTube or browsing — it became part of my daily workflow.

The biggest breakthrough was keeping work and personal life separate. Different password managers, extensions, and accounts all had their own space. Chrome handled this brilliantly with profiles, and it was a game-changer. On top of that, tab groups made it possible to corral the chaos: CMS tools in one group, Raygun tabs in another, GitHub in another — all collapsible and easy to manage.

Then came tab sync, which tied it all together. At the time I had three main devices — a home desktop, a work desktop, and a personal laptop. Being able to pick up exactly where I left off, no matter which one I was on, felt seamless. Chrome was no longer just a default browser; it was a productivity tool.

Looking back, this is where things escalated. Once I saw how much a browser could streamline my workflow, I started chasing that feeling everywhere. Chrome was good — really good — but I couldn’t help wondering if something else out there might be even better. That’s when I found my next favorite browser.

## Built for the few, not the funnel

Around this time I was diving deeper into developer communities, and there was a constant buzz about a new browser called Arc. It wasn’t pitched as “just another browser.” Instead, it was opinionated — made for people who treated their browser as a core tool, not just a gateway to the internet. After leaning so heavily on Chrome for workflows, that idea hooked me.

At first, Arc was Mac-only, which kept me on the sidelines. But then they opened a private Windows beta, and thanks to a coworker who got access (and invited me), I finally got to try it. My first impressions were mixed: a little rough, but undeniably exciting.

### The “wow” phase

Arc felt reimagined, not just iterated. Vertical tabs alone made a huge difference — I could actually read tab names again. Pinned tabs, pinned groups, and essential tabs made sense immediately. Arc sync was seamless, workspaces had proper keybinds, extensions stayed local to each workspace… it all clicked together beautifully.

Some features felt genuinely magical.

- Live folders: GitHub was the killer one, automatically surfacing PRs when I had reviews waiting.

- Tab sync: even smoother than Chrome’s.

- Video pop-outs: a miniplayer that followed you across tabs, not just apps.

- New tab menu: no blank tab spam — just a clean overlay until you searched.

- Essential tabs: Gmail, Calendar, whatever you needed, always within reach.

- Even the AI tab organization — a bit gimmicky, sure — still felt like the future.

For a while, life really was sunshine and rainbows.

### Mac envy

During this period I was doing a lot of cross-platform work with MAUI, so I often had a MacBook nearby. Naturally, I tried Arc there too — and that’s when reality hit. The macOS version was miles ahead. Extra polish, glance windows for email and calendars, more color schemes, just… more of everything. It was exciting and crushing at the same time. Windows Arc felt like the understudy, always waiting for features that never quite arrived.

### The slow fade

Then the updates slowed down. Weekly features stopped. Performance began to drag. And finally, The Browser Company announced Arc 2.0. Their reasoning? Arc had built a cult following of power users, but that wasn’t enough to sustain a VC-backed company with no revenue. They needed something for the masses.

That was the beginning of the end. The features I loved were still there, but the magic was gone — and worse, the browser itself got clunky. Even local development became painful: typing localhost:6001 and hitting enter would often trigger a Google search before Arc realized it was a URL. Too smart for its own good, and far too slow.

### Moving on

I kept using Arc for a while out of loyalty, but eventually curiosity won. I knew I loved what Arc tried to be, but without momentum, it wasn’t enough. That’s when I landed on Zen — amusingly around the same time TheoT3 posted his own “Arc betrayed me, I’m moving to Zen” video. At work, it turned into a running joke that I was just following him around. In reality, I had gotten there myself — but the timing couldn’t have looked worse.
